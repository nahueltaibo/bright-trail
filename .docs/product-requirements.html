1. Vision and principles

Help a child reach the official standard for their grade. We start with the Ontario curriculum only. Find what they don't know yet, teach it with short, fun content, then confirm they mastered it. For some kids this closes a few gaps; for others it is the whole curriculum.

  • Works with or without a school. In remote or under-resourced areas BrightTrail may be the only source of learning. It must teach every skill of a grade from scratch, not only patch gaps.
  • Curriculum-grounded. Every exercise maps to a real expectation (e.g. Ontario Grade 3 Math, strand B1.2).
  • Small and fun. Sessions of 5–15 minutes. Content feels like play.
  • Prove it. A skill counts as learned only after a mastery check, and again after a spaced review.
  • Parent in control. A parent owns the account. The child uses a kid-safe space.

Onboarding inputs

  • Birthdate, not grade. Ontario places kids by calendar birth year: a child starts Grade 1 in September of the year they turn 6. Grade = school-year start year − birth year − 5. The parent can correct it for a child held back or skipped ahead.
  • City confirms the child is in Ontario. Store the city, not a precise location.
  • Interests (dinosaurs, soccer, space) theme the content. They do not change what is taught.
  • Subjects to focus on (optional). The parent or child can pick; otherwise we assess all core subjects.
  • Language of instruction (e.g. English, French immersion).

4. Mastery loop

Each skill moves through these states for each learner.

stateDiagram-v2
  [*] --> Unknown: skill at or above grade
  [*] --> Assumed: skill below starting grade
  Assumed --> Mastered: confirmed by probe or use
  Assumed --> Gap: probe failed
  Unknown --> Mastered: assessment shows strong
  Unknown --> Gap: assessment shows weak
  Gap --> Learning: nugget started
  Learning --> Mastered: check passed
  Learning --> Gap: check failed twice
  Gap --> Gap: prerequisite gap found first
  Mastered --> ReviewDue: spaced-review timer
  ReviewDue --> Mastered: review passed
  ReviewDue --> Learning: review failed
  • Failing a check twice triggers a different nugget format or a prerequisite skill.
  • Review intervals grow after each pass (e.g. 2 days, 1 week, 3 weeks).
  • The learner model updates mastery probability after every answer, not only at the end of a check.

5. Curriculum engine

The hardest data problem. Official curricula are long PDFs and web pages written for teachers. We turn them into a machine-readable skill graph once per framework version.

  S[Official sources
Ontario curriculum PDFs and site] --> P[AI extraction

  P --> N[Normalize
one skill per testable expectation]
  N --> R[AI proposes prerequisites
and difficulty]
  R --> H[Second-model review
educator sign-off later]
  H --> G[(Skill graph
versioned)]
  G --> X[Item bank seeding]
  • Ontario only. Kindergarten Program, then Grades 1–8: Math, Language, Science and Technology, Social Studies, French.
  • Versioned. Ontario revises subjects on different years. A learner stays pinned to the version their school uses.
  • Reusable nuggets. Nuggets attach to a skill, not to a framework. Most skills are the same across curricula, so another framework can later map its expectations onto existing skills and reuse their nuggets.
  • AI review first. A second model checks the extracted structure. Educator review comes later.

Skill graph

Skills form a tech tree. A skill unlocks only when all its prerequisites are mastered or assumed.

flowchart LR
  A["Count to 50
Grade 1 · assumed"] --> C["Add within 20
Grade 1 · assumed"]
  B["Place value tens
Grade 1 · assumed"] --> D["Add 2-digit numbers
Grade 2 · assumed"]
  C --> D
  D --> E["Add 3-digit numbers
Grade 3 · learning"]
  E --> F["Multiply as repeated addition
Grade 3 · locked"]
  • Assumed mastery. A child who starts in Grade 3 gets every earlier skill marked assumed: approved without proof.
  • Walk back on a gap. When a skill fails, probe its prerequisites. An assumed prerequisite that fails becomes a gap, and the walk continues to its own prerequisites until a mastered skill is found.
  • Confirm by use. Passing a check on a skill confirms its assumed prerequisites as mastered.

6. Assessment and diagnosis

Initial assessment

  • Adaptive. Start at the learner's grade. Step down on wrong answers to find the floor, step up on right ones.
  • Short and split. One subject per session, 10–15 minutes. Young kids may need several sessions.
  • Prerequisite-aware. If a Grade 3 skill fails, probe its Grade 2 prerequisites before marking it a gap.
  • Game-like, low stakes. No "test" language. No visible scores during the assessment.

Measuring knowledge

TechniqueUse
Item Response Theory or Elo ratingCalibrate item difficulty and learner ability on the same scale.
Bayesian Knowledge TracingTrack probability of mastery per skill; handles guesses and slips.
Misconception taggingWrong answers map to a known misconception (e.g. "adds denominators"). Drives targeted nuggets.
Behavior signalsResponse time, hints used, retries. Flags guessing or frustration.

Item types

  • Auto-graded: multiple choice, number entry, drag-and-drop, matching, ordering, tap-the-picture.
  • Reading and listening: short passage or audio with comprehension questions.
  • Open responses (writing, explaining reasoning): AI-graded with a rubric. Use sparingly and with confidence thresholds.
  • Speech (for early readers): read-aloud with speech recognition. Later phase.

Generation rules

  • Grounded. Every prompt includes the skill text, grade, examples, and known misconceptions.
  • Verified. Math answers checked by a deterministic solver. Other subjects checked by a second model pass.
  • Audio-first. Every nugget, item and prompt is narrated, so a child can learn without an adult who reads. The child can turn narration off and read instead.
  • Age-fit. Reading level matched to grade. Younger kids get picture-heavy content.
  • Reuse first. Generated content is cached and shared across learners. Only theme and name change. This controls cost and allows later review of popular content.
  • No PII in prompts. The child's name is inserted after generation, never sent to the model.
  • Model tiers. A strong model (e.g. Claude Sonnet 5.5) for nuggets; a fast model (e.g. Claude Haiku 5.5) for validation and hints.

9. Safety, privacy, compliance

Children's data is the top legal risk. Design for privacy from day one; retrofitting is expensive.
  • Laws to meet: PIPEDA and Québec Law 25 (Canada), COPPA (US), GDPR and UK Age Appropriate Design Code (if offered in Europe). MFIPPA applies if Ontario school boards adopt it.
  • Verifiable parental consent before any child data is collected.
  • Data minimization: first name, birthdate, city. No photos, no precise location, no last name.
  • No open chat with the AI for young kids. Any free-text input is moderated and never stored longer than needed.
  • Data residency: keep Canadian learner data in Canada if targeting schools.
  • Content safety: every generated asset passes moderation. Kids can flag content; flags go to the review tool.
  • Healthy engagement: rewards for effort and mastery, not time spent. Parent-set session limits. No ads, no dark patterns.
  • Parent rights: view, export, and delete all child data.

12. Decisions and risks

Decisions

QuestionDecision
Who pays?Free for about the first year. Pricing comes later, so charging stays possible.
Which age range first?Ages 6–10 (Grades 1–5).
Who confirms grade progression?The app tracks knowledge per skill and approximates a grade from it. No certificates at first.
Who helps a child with no adult who reads?Audio-first design. The child can turn narration off and read instead.
How much educator review?None at first. AI validation is enough. Revisit later.
Mobile or web first?Mobile app for phone and tablet first, on a stack that can be reused for web.

Risks

  • AI errors teach a wrong fact. Mitigation: solvers, second-model review, kid flagging. Educator sampling later.
  • Curriculum parsing quality. Without educator sign-off, a wrong prerequisite or a missed expectation can go unnoticed. Mitigation: second-model review per framework version.
  • Assessment fatigue in young kids. Mitigation: short, split sessions and continuous assessment during quests.
  • Engagement drop after novelty. Mitigation: quests, avatars, interest themes, parent nudges.
  • Unit cost of generation. Mitigation: library reuse, cheaper models for validation, pre-generation off-peak.
